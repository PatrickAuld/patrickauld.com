title: 'Watches'

A watch can tell you more than the time. It can make the passage of a minute feel different, suggest the light outside, or give an approaching moment a little gravity.

These are my experiments in making time visible. I’m interested in what a screen makes possible: shadows with no physical object to cast them, patterns that seem to move faster than their parts, and a dial that changes its character over the day.

## Sundial II — time in the light

![Sundial II: an ivory circular dial with a floating chord, unequal hour and minute endpoints, and a recessed rim.](/watches/sundial-ii.webp)

Two points mark the hour and minute. A chord connects them, and its shadow follows the Sun above Alameda, California. The result is a clock with two rhythms: the geometry tells the time, while the light gives the day its shape.

The chord can float above the dial as a line or rise from it as a wall. A shaded rim gives the surface a sense of depth. Through twilight, the ivory dial fades toward quiet blue; at night, the solar shadows disappear.

The location is fixed to Alameda. This is a small portrait of a place as much as an instrument for telling time.

## Radial Moiré — motion between the lines

![Radial Moiré: fine circular ink gratings produce broad interference bands beneath outlined watch hands.](/watches/radial-moire.webp)

Fine lines overlap. One plate turns beneath another, and a larger pattern emerges between them. The apparent movement belongs to the interference: a small rotation can send broad bands flowing around the dial.

Three mechanisms explore that effect: a pinwheel, petals that bloom and fold, and arcs streaming between two poles. Paper-and-ink palettes change the mood while the hands remain legible above the pattern.

A still image captures the engraving. The moving preview reveals the other half of the design.

[Watch Radial Moiré move →](https://patrickauld.github.io/watches/faces/radial-moire/)

## Crosshatch — an approaching moment

![Crosshatch: pale engraved lines on a black dial form a countdown wedge toward the next half hour.](/watches/crosshatch.webp)

Most watches tell you where you are in the hour. Crosshatch asks how close you are to the next beginning.

A wedge of engraved lines narrows toward the next :00 or :30. In the final five minutes, its color warms and its pulse quickens. At the boundary, the dial flashes with crosshatching, then settles again.

It began with a practical question: could a glance make an upcoming meeting feel obvious? The remaining area becomes a visual measure of the time left. This face is still a draft.
